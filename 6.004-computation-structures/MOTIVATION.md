# Why 6.004 exists

## The problem

By the end of 6.002 you can explain, from transistor physics up, why a MOSFET behaves like a voltage-controlled switch. That's it — a switch. One switch, on or off, doesn't compute anything.

Keep one thing in mind going in, because it will matter a lot in Act 4 below: a wire's voltage is a continuous, analog quantity at every instant of continuous time — nothing about physical reality is ever discrete. "Digital" is not a different kind of physics; it's a decision to *interpret* a continuous voltage as one of two logical values, once it's had time to settle into a range we've agreed to call 0 or 1. That's a discretization of the **value** (the codomain), not of **time** (the domain) — time is still flowing continuously underneath every gate you're about to build. The actual problem 6.004 exists to solve is this:

> **How do you go from a pile of on/off switches to a machine that can run any program you hand it?**

Nobody wires up switches one at a time to build a calculator, let alone a computer. There has to be a sequence of abstraction layers, each one hiding the mess underneath, so that "run this program" doesn't require thinking about individual transistors at all. 6.004 is the course that builds that ladder, rung by rung, and shows you *why each rung is necessary* — not just what it looks like once built. Skip this course and "the computer runs my code" stays magic. Every later systems course (6.033, 6.191, 6.858, 6.824) assumes you've climbed this ladder once and know where the bodies are buried.

## What to expect

The 31 topics in the README aren't a flat list — they're six acts, each one solving the problem the previous act's abstraction can't:

1. **Combinational logic (topics 1–7).** Switches → Boolean functions → gates → useful building blocks (muxes, adders, ALUs). By the end, you can compute *any* fixed function of your current inputs. The gap this leaves: nothing here has memory. Feed the same inputs in tomorrow, you get the same outputs — there's no way to depend on *history*.
2. **Sequential logic (topics 8–10).** Bistability, latches, flip-flops. This is the rung that adds memory — a circuit that can disagree with what its inputs currently say, because it remembers what happened before. The gap: a flip-flop remembers one bit. A useful machine needs to remember *which step of a procedure it's on*.
3. **Finite state machines (topics 11–13).** Combinational logic + memory, wired into a loop, gives you a machine that can implement *any* fixed procedure with a finite number of steps. The gap: an FSM's behavior is baked into its wiring at design time. You can't hand it a new procedure without rewiring it.
4. **Timing (topics 14–16).** Here's where the continuous-voltage/continuous-time fact from the intro stops being a footnote and becomes the whole problem. Every gate takes a nonzero, continuous amount of time to settle its output into a valid "0" or "1" range after its inputs change — combinational logic never produces an instantaneous answer, only an eventually-correct one. Sequential logic then makes this worse: a flip-flop's next state depends on its current state, so if you don't fix *when* "current" ends and "next" begins, the whole circuit has no well-defined notion of before/after — just a continuously evolving voltage with no seams cut into it. The clock signal is the fix: it's a second signal whose job is to discretize *time itself*, marking out specific instants (edges) at which every flip-flop in the circuit agrees to sample the (by-then-settled) voltage around it and call it valid data. Nothing here has anything to do with 0s and 1s being "naturally" discrete in time — they aren't; the clock manufactures the discreteness so that "state at step $n$" is even a meaningful sentence. (This is also your first real encounter with sampling a continuous signal at discrete instants — the same idea 6.003 formalizes later as continuous-time vs. discrete-time signals.)
5. **Datapath and the processor (topics 17–22).** This is the resolution to the FSM gap: build one FSM (the control unit) that doesn't implement the procedure directly, but instead *reads a description of the procedure* (an instruction) and reconfigures a shared pile of combinational + sequential hardware (the datapath) accordingly, one instruction at a time. This is the actual trick that makes a computer "general-purpose" — the hardware doesn't change, only the instructions fed to it do.
6. **Pipelining, memory hierarchy, and the assembly-level view (topics 23–31).** Once you have a processor that works, the rest of the course is making it fast (pipelining), feeding it (memory hierarchy — a single flip-flop-based register file doesn't scale to gigabytes), and giving humans a sane way to write the instructions it consumes (assembly, ISAs).

Notice the shape: every act exists because the previous act hit a wall. That's the actual narrative — not "here are eleven unrelated topics," but "here's a wall, here's what breaks it, here's the new wall that creates."

## Bridging questions

These aren't self-check questions — you won't be able to fully answer them with only 6.004 material. They're supposed to itch. Come back to them after finishing 6.004, and they'll point at what's missing.

1. Your single-cycle/pipelined processor executes one program's instructions faithfully, in order, one after another. Real machines run dozens of programs that all *appear* to execute at once, and none of them can read another's memory even though they share one physical processor and one physical RAM. Nothing in 6.004 stops program A's assembly from directly reading program B's memory address. What layer has to exist above the processor to make that isolation real, and what would it need to control that 6.004's hardware alone doesn't?
2. You built a cache under the assumption that one processor accesses one memory hierarchy. If two processors each have their own cache but share the same underlying memory, and both cache the same address, what happens the instant one of them writes to it? Nothing in your 6.004 cache design even detects that this situation exists — why not, and what would have to be added?
3. 6.004's timing discipline assumes a clean, stable clock and stable power reaching every flip-flop. What happens to your FSMs and pipeline registers if the clock is intermittent, power is limited, or the whole processor has to sleep and wake up without losing its place — the situation any battery-powered or sensor device is actually in? Is there anything in the 6.004 abstraction layer that already tells you the answer, or is this a genuinely new problem?
4. Your ISA and assembler trust that every instruction fetched is one the programmer intended to run. What would it take for a malicious program — one that's also just a valid sequence of instructions your processor is happy to execute — to make the processor do something its *own* programmer never wrote? Is that a hardware problem, a software problem, or does the question not even make sense yet with what 6.004 gives you?
